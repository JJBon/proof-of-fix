# Related-work register for the paper (proof-of-fix#158)

**What this is:** the sources for section 2 of [the outline](outline.md), "Background and related work", each checked and positioned against the mechanism. It was compiled on 2026-11-12 from public web pages only: no contact with any project, no sign-ups, no accounts.

**How to read it:**
- **Cite?** "yes" means the paper should cite it; "optional" means it supports a point but can be dropped; "no" means it is listed so a reviewer's "what about X?" has an answer.
- **Read status:**
  - **full text read:** the whole paper or document was fetched and read;
  - **abstract/summary only:** the abstract, the publisher's record or a summary was read, not the full text;
  - **project docs:** the project's own site, docs, code or forum posts were read.
- Bibliographic details were checked against the source itself (arXiv, the DOI's Crossref record, the venue's proceedings or the project's docs). Where a detail rests on a secondary source, the entry says so.
- **"The mechanism"** means the core design: an odd panel of k AI judges, open registration with a bond and TEE attestation, a seeded draw under caps, sealed votes, audits, challenges and slashing, deciding pass/fail on one fix for one issue, with the bounty paid to the first passing fix at merge. **"#186"** means the cycle pools, where a cycle-end AI panel scores each merged fix's merit in dollars.

## 1. Competitors and close prior work

**The short answer.** The core idea, AI judges deciding whether a pull request fixes an issue and a contract paying on their verdict, **already exists in several small projects.** None is a paper, none publishes accuracy or attack evidence, and none combines the mechanism's admission, attestation, diversity caps, audits and slashing (one, SealedBounty, has an attested single judge). So the paper can't claim to be the first to pay bounties on AI verdicts. It can claim:
1. the adversarial design of the judges' network;
2. the simulation evidence of what breaks and why;
3. the merit pools (#186).

**The closest group** builds on **GenLayer**, a chain whose validators each run an LLM and reach consensus on non-deterministic results ("Optimistic Democracy": a leader proposes, a stake-weighted committee commits then reveals votes, and anyone can appeal with a bond to a larger committee).
- **AutoBounty** (GenLayer hackathon, March-April 2026), **GH Bounty** (claims Solana mainnet), **GhBounty** (MVP) and an entry in GenLayer's 2024 hackathon ("Vogon Constructors", per GenLayer's post of 2024-08-27) all have LLM validators read an issue and a PR and decide whether the PR meets it, with escrow paying on the verdict.
- **MergeProof** (by GenLayer Labs) stakes submitters, bug hunters and attestors on a PR's quality. Today the bounty owner rules on bug reports; an LLM takes over in its roadmap.
- **How they differ from the mechanism:**
  - their judges are GenLayer's general validators, chosen by stake, with no qualification, no attestation and no per-family, per-host or per-TEE caps;
  - the judges read the PR and GitHub's CI status; they don't run the tests in an attested sandbox;
  - no random audits, no slash for a false verdict on a fix, no sealed lone-verdict hold;
  - no published accuracy, cost or attack evidence.
- **The paper should name GenLayer** as the general-purpose alternative: "LLM consensus as a chain feature". Its appeals to larger committees resemble the mechanism's challenges. The mechanism's answer is a specialised network whose judges are drawn and paid for one checkable question.

**Kleros now has AI jurors.** Its August and September 2026 updates (blog.kleros.io, project docs) report an "Agentic Commerce Court" created in Court V2 as a pilot for AI-agent jurors (its parameters were still being set in August), an "AI jurors experiment", a gate so a dispute that starts with AI jurors can be appealed to human jurors, plans for TEE inference, and an agent-related ERC drafted with GenLayer.
- **How close:** the nearest decentralised court moving to AI jurors, with jurors' stake at risk. It keeps Kleros's stake-weighted draw and Schelling coherence rewards, with humans as the final appeal.
- **What differs:** commerce disputes, not code fixes; no diversity caps, attestation or random audits; a pilot with no published results. The paper should cite it beside GenLayer.

## 2. Deep Funding

**What it is** (project docs: deepfunding.org; github.com/deepfunding; Buterin, "AI as the engine, humans as the steering wheel", vitalik.eth.limo, 2025-02-28):
- A dependency graph of Ethereum's open source: 31 seed repositories, 5,024 dependencies, 14,927 edges in the graph's README.
- Anyone submits weights for the edges (what share of a project's credit goes to each dependency), realistically made by AI models.
- A human jury answers a random sample of pairwise questions ("has A or B been more valuable to Ethereum's success?", and by how much).
- Each submission is scored by the squared error between its log-ratios and the jurors' log-multipliers. The final weights are the mix of submissions that best fits the jury (`scoring.py`, read in full). Money then flows along the weighted graph.
- Buterin calls the idea "distilled human judgment": humans give a few hundred bits; an open market of models extends them to every edge.

**What happened:**
- **The pilot** (submissions 2025-02-10 to 05-10). deepfunding.org says "rewards worth $220k": **$170k to repositories** by the winning models' weights and **$50k** to winning models and knowledge sharing.
  - Gitcoin's mechanism page (2026-03-05) gives a different split: $250k from Buterin, $170k to projects, $40k to the best model and $40k for open-source submissions. Its visual guide says $80k in prizes.
  - **No published record of the final payouts or winners was found.** Gitcoin's page says only "initial rounds" are complete and that performance at scale "remains to be demonstrated".
- **The jury:**
  - 611 judgments by 51 jurors between 2024-12-24 and 2025-03-31 (Kronosapiens Labs' analysis, 2025-08-03);
  - jurors chose the same project 63 % of the time for direct dependencies and 89 % for child dependencies;
  - "about 650 comparisons from 45 jurors over about nine months", with jurors overstating differences about 3× (a median multiplier of 5× against a reconciled 1.8×) and disagreeing on order about 40 % of the time (Niemerg, Gitcoin forum, 2026-03-24).
- **The move to markets:** Yeung, Lesaege (Kleros's lead author) and Mehta (ethresear.ch, 2025-09-25) replace the prize contest with **prediction markets on the weights**, settled by jury spot checks on a few markets chosen for volatility.
  - Their reason: the contest had a sybil problem (submit many models and win with whichever fits).
  - The trial had 45 repositories and about $44k of liquidity; about $6k was the "cost to obtain information".
  - **GG24's Deep Funding round** channels $350k over 98 repositories through these markets. It ends with GG25. In January 2026 it had about 500 evaluations from 50 evaluators and about $1,500 of trades (Mehta, Gitcoin forum, 2026-01-11).
- **Critiques:**
  - "The Legitimacy Engine" (HackMD, undated, author not stated): a credentialed jury; 64.5 % of comparisons had one annotation; "selecting a winning model is statistically meaningless when your validation data is this noisy"; recipients can't trace why they got an amount.
  - Gitcoin's own page lists graph gaming, a narrow idea of impact, jury bias and model opacity.
  - Model builders reported that dependency PageRank was anti-correlated with jury weights (Spearman −0.13), and that simple models beat complex ones on so few labels (Gitcoin and Ethereum Magicians forums).

**Against the core mechanism:** different questions, and the difference is the point.
- **Deep Funding answers a judgment question:** what share of credit does a dependency deserve? No ground truth exists, so humans are the reference, and models are graded by how well they predict humans.
- **The mechanism answers a checkable question:** does this fix resolve this issue, with these tests? Ground truth largely exists (tests, the issue, the diff). So the judges can be audited against outcomes, slashed for false verdicts, and measured on real fixes (`reports/judges-v1.md`).
- **The two put humans in different places.** Deep Funding keeps a human jury as the root of trust and uses AI to scale it. The mechanism removes the human jury: trust comes from independent model families, attestation, audits and stakes, and from challenges that anyone can bring.
- **Timing differs too.** Deep Funding pays retroactively, by importance, to whole repositories. The mechanism pays per fix, at merge, to whoever fixed it.

**Against #186's merit panel:** this is the real overlap, and the paper should say so.
- **Both turn sparse, noisy judgments of value into dollar allocations** across many contributions. Both use AI to produce the values, and both calibrate against a small trusted reference: Deep Funding's jury spot checks; #186's reference fixes with recorded merits.
- **Differences:**
  - **Unit:** #186 scores merged fixes inside one cycle and one repository's pool; Deep Funding weights repositories and dependency edges.
  - **Who judges:** #186's panel is the network's judges, drawn after the cycle, with sealed scores and the median per fix. Deep Funding's values come from open submissions (or market traders), graded by a human jury.
  - **Correction:** #186 lets anyone with a claim on the pool challenge a merit before the split, and stakes reporters on claimed importance. Deep Funding has spot checks, and in its market version a bond for proposing a new edge. It has no challenge by funders.
  - **Money's source:** #186 splits funders' own coin, capped at merit, with the leftover returned to each funder. Deep Funding distributes a sponsor's grant.
- **What #186 should take from Deep Funding's record:**
  - **Calibration data is the bottleneck.** Deep Funding struggled to get a few hundred consistent human judgments, and its jurors were inconsistent in scale. #186's reference fixes will face the same problem; #160 should measure merit error against them before any parameter is fixed.
  - **Open model contests invite sybil fitting.** That is the reason Deep Funding moved to markets. #186 draws its panel from bonded judges rather than open submissions, which avoids that particular attack.
  - **Legibility.** Critics' main complaint is that recipients can't trace an amount. #186's per-fix median, its published reasons and its challenge path answer that, and the paper can say so.

## 4. Gaps and open questions

- **The competitors move fast.** Most of section 1 is from 2026. Check GenLayer's mainnet, GH Bounty, Verdikta and Gittensor again just before submission, and say "as of" in the paper.
- **No competitor publishes evidence,** so there's nothing to compare the simulation's numbers with. The paper's real-system data is the determinism and zkTLS work; the paid judge test (#160) would be the first accuracy record on real fixes in any of these systems.
- **Deep Funding's results aren't published.** The pilot's final payouts, winning models and their error against the jury weren't found. The paper should cite the design and the jury analyses, not outcomes.
- **Most entries were read at abstract level.** Before submission, read in full at least: Zhou et al. (TSE 2021), Conti et al. (NBER), Wang, Pradel and Liu (ICSE 2026), Verga et al. (PoLL), Shi et al. (CCS 2024), GitInject, and the oracle papers in section 3.2.
- **Wording to fix elsewhere in the outline** (not changed here, since only section 2 was in scope):
  - section 6 says zkTLS has "a 4 KB request cap". That is the TLSNotary reference notary server's default (`max_sent_data` 4,096), not a protocol limit;
  - section 7's TEE trust point can cite TEE.fail, WireTap and Battering RAM (physical interposers forging attestation quotes).
- **Kleros and GenLayer are drafting an agent ERC together.** If it lands before submission, the paper should say how the mechanism relates to it.
- **Two sources failed to load:** OpenAI's SWE-bench Verified post (403) and Optimism's 2021 post (403). Their facts come from search snippets and copies.
- **Not covered yet:**
  - the peer-review literature on code review (for example, Bacchelli and Bird, ICSE 2013) and on review load from AI-written PRs;
  - mechanism-design work on contests and first-to-solve prizes, which the race resembles;
  - any legal or institutional work on algorithmic arbitration (AAA-ICDR's AI arbitrator is in the market survey, not here).
- **Open questions for the outline:**
  - Should section 2 name the GenLayer bounty projects individually, or as one class? They're prototypes, but reviewers may know them.
  - Does the paper position #186 against Deep Funding in section 2, or in section 7 as an open problem (calibration data)?
